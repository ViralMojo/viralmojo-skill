# Iteration moves

Covers: the two ways to think about how big a swing to take, the high-confidence moves that recombine what's already proven, the broader menu, and the order to apply them in.

## Two ways to think about swing size

**The optimization hierarchy** — the order to work through, from the ad's broad category down to who's in it: **theme** (the broad territory the message lives in — exhaust this first) → **angle** (the specific emotional or motivational lens inside that theme) → **format** (the delivery vehicle — once angles inside the theme are exhausted) → **avatar** (the persona or creator demographic — change this last; the platform's own delivery targeting is usually sophisticated enough to find the right audience once the right pieces are in place, and swapping avatar too early is really just making a new ad). This is the default sequence; let the data override it when the diagnosis clearly points elsewhere.

**The signal-change ladder** — from loudest to quietest, how big a swing each individual move reads as: **format** (static to video, video to high-production — changes how the ad gets classified and served) → **vehicle** (founder story, testimonial, listicle, reaction — same message, different entry point) → **persona** (a creator swap — shifts who the platform targets and how the audience relates to it) → **hook** (a new opening can read as effectively new creative while the converting body stays intact) → **messaging** (script and copy tweaks — the smallest signal change, for fine-tuning, not for breaking into new territory).

Use both together: the hierarchy says what order to attack in; the ladder says how loud a specific move reads once you've picked it. Neither ranks leverage — quiet is not small: measured by how often a single variable moves the result, the hook is usually the highest-leverage move there is, because it changes who stops.

## The high-confidence moves — lead with these when they fit the diagnosis

Each of these recombines something already proven rather than betting on something new, so the risk sits in the seam, not the components.

**Hook-stitch.** Lift the proven hook from one winning ad and splice it onto the body of another. Best when several winners exist and there's a clean hook-to-body pairing with compatible pacing and energy at the cut point. Worst when only one winner exists, or the two sources clash in tone at the join.

**Same-script, new face.** Send the proven script to a new creator — same demographic for a fresh face, or a different demographic to reach a new archetype. Best when the script is converting and the creator is fatiguing, or a new demographic needs testing. Worst when the script itself is the bottleneck, or the current creator is clearly the strongest element.

**Format swap.** Rebuild the winning message in a different format — video to static, static to video, video to carousel. The loudest move on the signal ladder. Best when the message converts and hasn't been tested in this format yet, or fresh distribution is the goal. Worst when the message itself isn't converting yet, or the brand has already tried it across formats with no lift.

**Desire swap.** Identify the human desire the ad currently runs on — belonging, status, curiosity, security, achievement, and the rest — and rebuild the same product, format, and structure around a different one. The only high-confidence move that targets the emotional engine itself. Best when the current desire is clearly identifiable and the brand hasn't tested others. Worst when the current desire is the brand's whole positioning, or the ad's desire can't be named clearly enough to know what to swap to.

**Highlight mashup.** Combine the strongest moments from multiple winners into one compilation, stripping individual CTAs so it flows as one piece. Best when several winners exist and their best moments genuinely flow together. Worst with only one winner, or moments that don't sit well back to back.

**Hook-iteration.** Change only the first three seconds, keeping the proven body untouched. Best when the body is converting (high watch-through or high conversion once someone's past the open) but the click-through or the open itself is the bottleneck — or the ad is fatiguing while the core message still works. Worst when the body itself is what's losing people; a new hook only delays that drop-off.

## The broader menu

Reach for these when the diagnosis doesn't fit a high-confidence move, or those have already been run.

**Messaging-angle iteration.** Keep the visuals and structure mostly intact; rewrite the script around a different angle, awareness stage, or framing. Best when the visuals work but the message isn't converting. Worst when the message is already converting and the higher-confidence moves haven't been tried yet.

**Audience iteration.** Creator changes that go beyond a same-script reshoot — a different demographic entirely, an authority voice instead of a peer voice, a different persona variant. Best when the goal is genuinely expanding into a new segment. Worst when the current creator is clearly driving performance, or a same-script reshoot hasn't been tried first.

**Static headline iteration.** On a static ad, test a different headline structure while the layout and visual stay put. Best when the headline is the diagnosed bottleneck on a static. Worst when the visual is the actual problem, or the current headline is converting.

**Edit and pacing iteration.** Low-risk refreshes — pacing, a cold open, a length trim or extension, a music or caption change. Best as a quick, low-effort complement alongside a bigger swing, or a fast refresh on a long-running winner. Worst as the only move when the account needs a real plateau-breaker.

## Choosing the set

In order: match the diagnosis first — drop anything that doesn't address the actual bottleneck. Protect what's converting; don't propose the working element's replacement as a first move. Lead with a high-confidence move when it genuinely fits, but never just because it's historically strong. Check whether two existing winners can simply be recombined before recommending anything that needs fresh production. Mix at least one low-effort move in when it makes sense for the diagnosis, and match overall swing size to spend — heavy spend usually means a saturated audience that needs a bigger swing, while an ad still scaling quickly is better served by smaller, lower-risk moves that protect its trajectory. When recommending three or more, favor moves from different levels of the hierarchy over three variations on the same lever, and don't repeat a move this ad's history has already exhausted — build on what those results taught instead.
