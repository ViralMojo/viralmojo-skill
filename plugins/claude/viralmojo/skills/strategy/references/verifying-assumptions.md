# Verifying an assumption

Covers: when a call's confidence genuinely hinges on something unverified, how to settle it honestly rather than quietly assume it or quietly avoid it — turn it into a bet, set the bar before looking, check a few real sources, and resolve to one of four honest states.

## When this applies

Not every gap needs this. Most of what "rests on" a call is fine to just name and move past — the user came for direction, not a research project. Reach for this specifically when the call's confidence turns on one real, checkable question, and the answer would genuinely change the recommendation — a competitor's new claim that might undercut the messaging call, a persona the account data keeps surfacing that was never named, a "why do people actually switch" question the whole product-priority call rests on. If settling it wouldn't change what to recommend, it's not worth this — just name it as a limitation and move on.

## Turn it into a bet, not a question left open

A vague worry ("maybe this segment matters") isn't checkable. State the actual prediction plainly, as a bet — "this segment converts at a lower CPA than the stated ICP, which would make it worth a deliberate push" — and mark it as exactly that: a bet, not yet a fact. If no real evidence could come back and prove it wrong, it isn't a real bet yet — sharpen it until it is.

## Set the bar before looking

Decide, before pulling anything, what result would count as **confirmed**, what would count as **refuted**, what would count as **genuinely mixed**, and what would count as **not enough to say either way**. Writing this down first is what keeps the verdict honest — a bar written after seeing the evidence bends to fit whatever came back.

## Check a few real, complementary sources — not one, and not a research project

Pick two or three sources that each see a different angle of the same question — the account's own data, customer evidence or reviews, a competitor read, whatever the specific question actually needs. One source alone has a blind spot the others cover: the account can't speak to an opportunity it's never tried, so a question about whether something untested is real usually needs reviews or competitor evidence alongside it. The discipline is depth at the right level, not breadth for its own sake — read each source enough to actually answer, and stop; don't turn one question into a multi-step study with sub-analyses nobody asked for.

**Look for the case against it as hard as the case for it, in the same pass.** Most weak verdicts come from only having looked for confirmation. If the honest search for disconfirming evidence never happened, the verdict isn't earned yet.

## Resolve to one of four honest states

- **Confirmed** — the evidence clears the bar that was set.
- **Refuted** — the evidence clears the bar for no, plainly.
- **Mixed** — real evidence on both sides, roughly balanced. Not a soft yes — say it's mixed.
- **Not enough to say** — the sources didn't turn up enough to judge either way. Different from mixed: mixed means plenty of evidence pointing both ways, this means there wasn't enough evidence at all.

A forced "confirmed" because it closes the question neatly is worse than leaving it open — it becomes something the brand trusts that isn't actually earned, and every call built on it inherits the mistake.

## What happens to the finding

A **confirmed** or **refuted** result that changes something durably true about the brand gets written into wherever it actually belongs — brand context, personas, or the strategy call itself, rewritten to reflect it — not left to evaporate at the end of the conversation. A **mixed** or **not-enough** result is simply reported honestly in the moment; there's nothing durable yet to write down. Either way, say plainly what was found and what it changes, if anything.

Nothing here is permanent once written — if new evidence later contradicts a confirmed finding, that's just a fresh case of this same process, run again.
