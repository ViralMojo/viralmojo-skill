# Script approaches

Covers: why adaptation is the default and net-new is the rare fallback, how to build a brand's voice fingerprint, how to find and adapt a real reference, the source-driven ways a script gets built when a real thing is worth building it around, and the structural frameworks to fall back on when no reference exists.

## The core premise

A model writing a script cold, with no reference and no fingerprint, produces clean, written-sounding copy that dies the moment it's read aloud. So the default path is **adaptation**: find something real that already works — one of the brand's own ads, a competitor's, an organic breakout, a customer's own words — and rebuild it in the brand's actual voice. Writing net-new, from a structural framework with no real source behind it, is the fallback for when nothing worth adapting exists — never the first move, and never exempt from the same voice discipline once it's chosen.

## Building the voice fingerprint

A brand's voice is audible, not visible — it doesn't show up from skimming text, only from hearing scripts read aloud at real speed. Before writing anything, pull 10–15 of the brand's own winning scripts with full transcripts, sorted by spend and by hook rate — a script winning on both is the truest signal. If the account is thin, fall back to saved references in the swipe file or the strongest organic creators in the niche to hear the register before the account has enough of its own history.

Read every one of them out loud, and pull out:

- **Pacing** — sentence length, how much it jumps around, how fast it cuts between thoughts.
- **Diction** — the brand's actual recurring words and phrases, pulled verbatim, never approximated.
- **Signature moves** — the tics that repeat: personification, listing specifics, naming a body part or object directly, admitting the catch before someone else points it out.
- **Direct-address habit** — who the brand talks to, and how — peer-to-peer, blunt, formal, teasing.
- **CTA shape** — its actual closing pattern: how it reverses risk, how it signs off, how it reads out a link or code.

Then name what the brand's winners **never** do, even with no rule saying so — that absence is part of the voice too, and it holds as a real constraint on anything written afterward.

**The kept-mess rule.** A script this discipline produces should carry the brand's own real disfluencies — a natural "so," "okay," "honestly," placed where a thought genuinely turns, pulled from how this brand specifically talks, never generic filler sprinkled for effect. A script clean enough that a person could deliver it perfectly on the first take is a failure, the same class of failure as any tell in the writing-tells check — unless the brand's own voice genuinely runs polished, in which case say that plainly rather than defaulting to mess out of habit. Vary the pacing the same way real speech does: a two-word fragment next to a long run-on. Reading a script aloud and it landing in even, regular breaths is itself a sign it's too smooth.

## Finding a reference to adapt

Look in this order, and stop as soon as something genuinely fits:

1. **The swipe file** — the user has already curated what's worth referencing; check here first.
2. **The brand's own top-performing ads** — a proven structure from the account itself is the strongest possible source.
3. **A monitored competitor's creative** — a competitor ad's record is what the ad says, word for word: its hook, the text on the creative, the spoken transcript, the ad copy. Its breakdown names the awareness level, funnel stage, and creative format the ad was built for; the story, structure, and voice are yours to read from those words.
4. **The organic pool** — a saved video there carries the same: what it says word for word (its hook, the on-screen text, the spoken transcript), its breakdown labels, and its top comments, each marked where it is a question, a sign of purchase intent, or pushback.

Pick exactly **one**. Weigh audience fit, whether it actually performed, whether its pacing and length are close to the brand's own baseline, and whether every claim or proof beat in it can be swapped for something this brand can honestly say. Never blend two references into one adaptation — the skeleton has to come from a single source or the seams show.

## The adaptation method

The reference is already working — the job is never to redesign it, only to understand what makes it work and tell the same story in the brand's own voice.

1. **Deconstruct the reference into beats** — the timing, what's said, what's shown, and what job each beat does in the emotional arc.
2. **Extract the skeleton**: segment count, pacing, the proof type it uses (testimonial, demonstration, before-after, expert claim), where the emotional arc peaks, its hook framework, its CTA shape. This is what gets preserved — never altered.
3. **Load the brand's own baseline** from the voice fingerprint. Where the reference's pacing or directness sits outside the brand's own register, pull it back toward the brand's key — the skeleton stays, the voice doesn't drift.
4. **Substitute the brand's content into the structure, beat by beat** — same beat purpose, same rough word count per beat (the reference's pacing is itself part of what worked), same emotional intensity, and the brand's own proof swapped in for the reference's proof type. A proof type the brand can't honestly claim gets swapped for the nearest true equivalent (the compliance-substitution table below).
5. **Audit for drift** — read the adaptation back against the reference beat by beat. More beats, a different pace, a different arc than the original means it's drifted, and it gets pulled back before it ships.

## Where a script's material comes from

Something real and specific already exists, and the script is built to carry that specific thing rather than a generic framework. Check these before reaching for a structural fallback:

**A winning ad of the brand's own.** Treat it as separable proven parts rather than one fixed thing — keep the proven hook and write a new body under it, or keep the proven body and write a fresh hook into it, or pull one working piece from one winner and a different working piece from another and write new connective tissue between them. Whatever's new has to match the brand baseline so it doesn't clash with the part that's kept, and the read-aloud test here is a **seam audit**: read start to finish and listen for a pacing jolt or a voice shift at the join. If the actual result is just the same winner lightly tuned rather than something new, that's simpler work than this — a straight variation request, not a rebuild.

**A competitor's ad.** Adapt the structure exactly — same beats, same pacing, same proof position — but flip the buyer relationship rather than porting the rival's angle wholesale: where their ad shames the buyer into acting, this one can absolve; where their strongest beat rests on a claim only they can make, put the brand's own strongest claim in that same beat position instead. Every line of language comes from this brand's own customer evidence, never the rival's phrasing — only the shape is borrowed.

**A breakout organic video.** Weigh view and engagement against how long it's been up — high views on an old post is real signal, low views on a fresh one is just youth. Decide whether it's a full concept worth a complete adaptation, or just one piece — a hook, a format, a beat — worth lifting while the rest is built fresh. If the original worked because of a hack or trick, film the honest version of what the product actually does, and treat the hack as a one-time device rather than something to keep running — a dishonest demo that scales in paid spend costs more trust than it earns.

**A customer review.** A review is unscripted persuasion that already worked — the reviewer named their own trigger, voiced their own doubt, delivered their own verdict, in language a strategist wouldn't invent. Either open on the single sharpest line as the hook, or map the whole arc to beats in the order the reviewer actually lived it — trigger, doubt, verdict — never reordered for effect. Keep the phrasing verbatim; smooth only for readability, never toward marketing voice, or the credibility that made it worth using is gone.

**A real objection or comment.** A recurring, documented objection — a dupe accusation, a price pushback, plain skepticism — becomes the hook itself, staged as a real reply to a real comment. The rebuttal has to sound like a person answering, not a brand defending itself; an answer that reads defensive confirms the skeptic instead of disarming them. Never invent the comment — it has to be real and traceable back to customer evidence.

**A voice-of-customer convergence.** When many independent customers land on the exact same metaphor or phrase unprompted — the kind of thing the VoC store tracks explicitly as a cross-category convergence — that phrase has already stopped being one person's wording and become the category's own shared language. The script doesn't need to teach the audience a new frame; it just names the one they already carry. Speak in **only** that documented phrase — no synonyms, no "close enough" language the evidence doesn't actually support. One loud review isn't a convergence; check the mention count actually clears more than a single source before treating it as one.

**A persona at a specific point in their journey.** Fix two coordinates at once — which persona, and which lifecycle stage — because the same persona sounds different at different stages and the same stage sounds different across personas. Pull that persona's actual documented voice, not the brand's house voice, and write the whole script inside that specific intersection rather than for "the customer" in general.

**An idea-bank entry.** The idea's own justification — why it earned a slot — is the reason the script exists; build toward proving that reason out, not toward something else the logged idea never implied. How much building is left depends on the idea's form: a storyline nearly writes itself, a hook needs a body and close built around it, a format alone carries no message and needs one attached from real customer language before it's a script at all.

**A customer-invented ritual or demo.** When a community independently develops its own way of testing or using the product — a check, a comparison, a hack — filming it carries a trust no brand-built demo can match, because the audience already recognizes it as theirs. Keep the community's own name for it rather than rebranding it into marketing language, and run an honesty pass confirming the filmed outcome is the real one — if the honest result is modest, show the modest result.

**The brand's own founder story.** Only when it's genuinely documented — brand context leaves this slot honestly blank when nothing is found. If it's blank, say so and don't invent one; a founder story only carries trust when it's true and verifiable, and embellishing it is the fastest way to lose exactly the trust it's supposed to earn.

**A seasonal or calendar moment.** There's no standing calendar document to check — ask, or work from what the user says about how the brand usually shows up for this occasion. The main discipline is voice-fit per occasion: a gifting script is written in the voice of the person doing the buying, not the brand's own voice — a Father's Day script written for a daughter buying for her dad sounds like the daughter, not like the brand.

**An open lane or positioning gap.** When the brand's own competitive read has named a register or angle nobody else in the category is using, there's no reference to adapt — the lane is empty by definition — so the brand's own baseline sets the shape directly. The discipline here is not drifting back into the category's familiar, crowded register out of habit, which forfeits the exact thing the lane was worth claiming for.

**A credentialed expert.** A real person's credentials transfer trust the brand can't generate on its own — but only inside a real claims envelope: what a credential can honestly claim is narrower than what the brand might want said, so keep every line inside it. The stronger casting choice is the expert in a genuine, plain-language moment — explaining something the way they'd explain it to a patient or a friend — over a posed recitation of the resume; establish the credential once, then let them be a person.

**A strategy call resting on real evidence.** When a committed strategy call already carries strong evidence behind it, the script's job shifts from persuading to dramatizing — the case is already made, so let the evidence carry real weight told as a scene rather than recited as a number. A number lands harder inside a customer's actual experience than as a stat on its own.

Across all of these, the one universal discipline is that **the source material's authenticity is what's being sold on** — inventing, embellishing, or exaggerating any of it costs more than the source was ever worth. And every one of them still ends by matching the brand's own voice baseline and clearing compliance before it ships — the source picks the content, the brand's own fingerprint still decides how it sounds.

## When there's no reference — structural frameworks

Reach for these only when nothing above genuinely fits: the user explicitly wants something built from scratch, or the request is a deliberately untested angle with no precedent to lean on. Every framework below still pulls its language from real customer evidence — "no reference" means no single ad is being adapted, never permission to invent what people actually say.

**Problem/solution, five beats.** Hook (0–3s) → name and agitate the problem in the customer's own words, pushed to the behavioral or identity depth below rather than staying at the surface — and when the pain is diffuse, do the math for them: total the hours or dollars it actually costs until the frustration is a number they can weigh (3–10s) → reveal the mechanism, specifically — a vague "advanced formula" fails, a named specific reason it works doesn't (10–20s) → proof, matched to the kind of pain agitated — embarrassment calls for social proof, discomfort calls for relief-focused proof (20–35s) → one CTA, in recommendation voice (35s–end). Fits problem-aware or solution-aware audiences with a specific, real pain; a poor fit for lifestyle brands with nothing to agitate.

**Storytelling, five phases.** Open mid-story with a specific time marker and a specific person — never a generic opener (0–5s) → the conflict, what was actually at stake (5–15s) → the failed attempts and frustration (15–30s) → the moment of discovery (30–40s) → the resolution, with the product entering here, as the answer, never as the opening's hero (40s–end). Best for unaware audiences who need the emotional runway before the product shows up; poor fit for impulse categories with no patience for a setup.

**VSL (video sales letter), seven beats, for longer or high-consideration asks.** Hook with an open loop — an unfinished story, a numbered tease ("three things nobody tells you; the second one matters most"), a promised answer deliberately delayed; whatever shape it takes, every loop opened must close before the CTA (0–10s) → a lead matched to awareness stage — a discovery story for unaware, an educational lead for problem-aware, a false-hope or hidden-villain lead for solution-aware (10–30s) → the problem, pushed deep (30–60s) → the mechanism, built out fully (see below) (60–100s) → a proof stack mixing qualitative and quantitative proof (100–150s) → the offer, with real value framing (150–180s) → the close, with urgency and risk reversal (180s–end).

*Building the mechanism inside a VSL* (or any script where differentiation rests on how the product works): name the actual mechanism from the product itself — never invented — position it as its own category rather than a better version of something existing, state specifically how it works in a way that's accurate and defensible, and describe how the buyer actually experiences it day to day so the abstract mechanism becomes something ownable and concrete. If a competitor could credibly make the same claim, it isn't differentiated enough yet.

**Matched to awareness stage.** Unaware gets a discovery arc, product introduced late as the natural extension of what the story reveals. Problem-aware gets the problem acknowledged briefly, then education on the category of fix, then this product's specific mechanism. Solution-aware gets the failed attempt acknowledged first, then why this works when those didn't, then proof from people on a similar path. Product-aware skips straight to proof, objection-handling, and the offer. Most-aware just needs the offer restated with urgency. Don't force a stage's structure onto the wrong audience — an unaware audience opened with a bare offer, or a most-aware audience sat through unnecessary education, are both mismatches worth catching before writing.

**Matched to sophistication** (how many prior solutions this audience has already tried). High sophistication: open by naming the pattern of failed attempts, differentiate on mechanism specifically, and back it with specific, detailed proof — generic "advanced formula" language reads as one more version of what already didn't work. Low sophistication: name the problem plainly, explain the mechanism in a concrete, simple comparison, and let lighter social proof carry the close — this audience hasn't developed the skepticism yet that high-sophistication framing is built to answer.

**A named style**, when the user asks for one or the brand's voice strongly favors it: humorous (self-aware, setup-and-punchline, needs a genuinely permissive brand voice), testimonial (customer voice carries the whole script), demonstration (show over tell, needs real visual proof), educational (teach, then let the product extend naturally from the lesson), FUD (leans on a real risk — never a manufactured one, and compliance-sensitive by nature), comparison (a real side-by-side against the category or a named rival), lifestyle (aspirational scenes of the product inside the life the buyer wants). Pick one and hold it start to finish — a style that collapses partway through undoes the reason for choosing it.

**Emotional depth**, three levels, worth checking regardless of which framework is running: **surface** is the obvious physical complaint ("dandruff on my shoulders"); **behavioral** is what it stops someone from doing ("can't wear dark suits without brushing them off all day"); **identity** is what it makes them feel about themselves ("feels unprofessional, like people think I don't take care of myself"). Level 2 and 3 outperform Level 1 whenever the customer's own language actually supports going there — check reviews and comments for how deep customers already describe it before writing an identity-level line that isn't backed by anything real.

## What the script hands over

The script is the voice track. Deliver it VO-first, with a visual cue only where something specific must happen — a number or claim that has to land on screen, a specific asset that must appear, a beat whose timing depends on the visual. A script directed line by line reads as a contract and crowds out the editor's craft; on-screen text earns its place mostly where a number needs anchoring.

## The CTA

One CTA, always — more than one splits attention and neither lands. The whole job is to read as a recommendation from a person, not an instruction from an ad: a conditional recommendation ("if you're dealing with X, this is worth trying"), a personal endorsement ("this is the one I'd start with"), a low-friction nudge ("worth a look"), risk reversal when it's genuinely available (free returns, a guarantee), or an offer close with a real code or link. Match its register to whatever the body already established — a casual script doesn't earn a formal close — and echo whatever anchor the script built on: a script anchored on urgency closes on urgency, one anchored on quality closes on quality.

## The compliance-substitution table

When a reference or a real story makes a claim this brand can't honestly make, swap it for the nearest true equivalent rather than dropping the beat:

| The reference claims | Swap in |
|---|---|
| A before/after result | A routine, or a small, real, observable win |
| A medical or health outcome | A sensory or experience claim |
| A price-anchored comparison | Value framing |
| Controversy or a direct competitor callout | Curiosity |

This runs the same whether the claim came from a competitor's ad, a customer's own review, or an expert's mouth — a claim a customer or an expert actually said still gets swapped if the brand can't defend it standing behind it.

And in health-adjacent categories, certain outcome verbs never ship bare — *regulate*, *cure*, *treat*, *reverse*, *prevent*, *eliminate* — they take a softener (*helps*, *supports*, *may*) or get swapped per the table above.

Every script, adapted or net-new, still clears the writing-tells check before it ships — same bar as headlines and hooks.
