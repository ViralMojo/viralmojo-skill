# Headline patterns

Covers: studying the account's own corpus first, where a headline's material should come from before reaching for a structure, the brand-type/awareness/sophistication/depth decisions that pick a structure, and the structural pattern library itself.

## Study the running corpus first

Before writing a single headline, look at what the brand's own connected ad account has already run — the top-spend and top-performing ads' headlines and on-screen text. Read them yourself and group them into families by eye — there's no precomputed tag to query, so this is a reading pass, not a lookup. Adapting a structure this account has already proven beats inventing a fresh one: a headline never measured against what actually runs here is the one likeliest to sound written rather than spoken. If the account has no history yet, say so and write from the structural patterns below instead of pretending a baseline exists.

Pull the competitor's longest-running hooks and headlines too, when a competitor set is monitored — a claim a rival can't make is an angle, and a rival's own framing is something to counter with a spec they don't have.

Once you have both, extract the brand's actual fingerprint from its own winners — length, casing and punctuation habits, how it uses numbers, which register it runs in — and check every candidate against the writing-tells check using that fingerprint as the bar, not a generic notion of good copy.

## Where the headline comes from — check these before reaching for a structure

A headline grounded in something real beats a clever structure built on air. In order:

1. **Adapt a proven headline.** The account has already run something that fits this brief — swap the content, keep the structure.
2. **Lift a voice-of-customer line.** A phrase from the VoC store that's already close to headline-ready, used close to verbatim.
3. **Answer a real comment.** A recurring objection or question from the ad comments (surfaced through `performance-reading`) — name it in the customer's own words, then resolve it in the headline.
4. **Counter a competitor's gap.** A claim built from what a monitored competitor's ads and copy show they can't say.
5. **Build from a verified number.** A real count the brand can back — review counts, a study, a spec a competitor doesn't have. Never a number that isn't traceable to something real.

Only reach for the structural pattern library below when none of these five produce the line, or the brief genuinely needs a fresh structure.

## The brand-type decision — make this first

- **Problem/solution.** The customer already knows they have a problem and is looking for a fix. Headlines can be direct: name the problem, agitate it, promise the fix.
- **Lifestyle.** The customer isn't in pain — they're seeking identity, elevation, or self-expression. The headline's job is to make the right person feel "this is for me," not to solve anything.

Some brands straddle both across different products. Decide per the specific product and audience the headline is for, not the brand as a whole.

## Awareness stage

- **Unaware** — doesn't know the problem exists yet. Needs education and curiosity, not a solution claim.
- **Problem-aware** — knows something's wrong, doesn't know a fix exists. Name the problem clearly; create curiosity about the fix.
- **Solution-aware** — has tried other fixes that failed. Acknowledge the past attempt; differentiate.
- **Product-aware** — knows this product, is weighing it. Lean on proof and a direct reason to act now.
- **Most-aware** — ready to buy. Social proof, urgency, a direct call to act.

## Sophistication level (problem/solution only)

How many solutions has this customer already tried:

- **High** — three or more. Skeptical, burned before. Acknowledge the failed attempts, differentiate on mechanism, use contrarian or investment-framed angles.
- **Low** — zero to two, or just discovering the problem. Needs education and clarity — name the problem simply, ask the question, state the benefit plainly.

## Emotional depth — push past the surface

Three levels a problem/solution headline can sit at. Level 2 and 3 outperform Level 1:

- **Level 1 — surface.** The obvious physical problem. "Back pain."
- **Level 2 — behavioral.** What the problem stops them from doing. "Can't sit through your kid's game anymore."
- **Level 3 — identity.** How it makes them feel about themselves. "Feeling old before you are."

If a candidate is stuck at Level 1, push it to Level 2 or 3 before shipping it. The move that pushes it is asking *so what?* of the line until the answer stops being about the product and starts being about them — what the problem stops them doing, then how that makes them feel.

## The structural pattern library

Reach for these only after checking the five idea paths above. Pick 2–5 headlines, never the same structure twice — the point of a set is testing genuinely different approaches, not five synonyms of one idea.

**Works for both brand types**

| Pattern | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| Rejection-contrast | "Forget X." "Stop wasting money on Y." "Ditch the cheap version." |
| Metaphor-experience | "Like a gym membership, but for your finances." Makes an abstract benefit concrete. |
| Emotion-power-word | Strong verbs and emotive language intensifying a real claim — never a substitute for having a real claim. |

**Lifestyle only**

| Pattern | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| Identity-signal | "Built for people who [behavior]." Tribal, not universal. |
| Badge-value-status | "Premium picks." Exclusivity and status framing. |
| Viral-fomo-appeal | "Everyone's obsessed." Only when real social proof backs it — never invented buzz. |
| Reaction-discovery | "I almost cried." Captures the wow moment. |
| Convenience-simplification | "A week of lunches, ten minutes." |

**Problem/solution only**

| Pattern | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| Problem-naming-direct | Names the problem in the customer's own words, plainly. |
| Question-engagement | "Struggling to get through the afternoon?" |
| Before-after-contrast | "Twelve years of pain, gone in a week." Time-and-outcome contrast. |
| Promise-outcome | "No more waking up at 3am." A clear, specific outcome. |
| Duration-acknowledgment | Names how long they've been trying — for high-sophistication audiences who've tried everything. |
| Authority-proof | "Recommended by [real, verifiable authority]." |
| Alliteration-rhythm | Memorable sound patterns — "Bye Bye Bloating." Use sparingly; the rhythm should never replace the claim. |
| Parallel-structure | "No bloat. No crash. Just clean energy." Only when there are genuinely three distinct real claims to carry — see the AI-tells note on invented triads. |

## Choosing the recommended set

In order: study the corpus and check the five idea paths first. Lock the brand type. Filter by awareness stage. Filter by sophistication if problem/solution. Check the current emotional depth and push past Level 1 if the account has room to. Check the customer language actually available — don't reach for a testimonial-style pattern with no real quote behind it. Then pick 2–5 structurally different headlines, at least one of them a structure the brand isn't currently running if this is an iteration.

Every headline: under 10 words, a specific person feeling a specific emotion, works cold, fifth-grade reading level, and clears the writing-tells check before it ships.
