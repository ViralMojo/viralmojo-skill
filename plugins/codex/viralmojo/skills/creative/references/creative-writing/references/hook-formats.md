# Hook formats

Covers: the four elements of a hook, the brand-type/awareness/emotional-driver decisions that pick a format, the 18 formats themselves, and how to choose a final set. A hook is the first three seconds of a video ad — most of what determines whether the rest gets watched happens here.

## Study the account's hooks first

Before writing, read the brand's own top-performing ads for what hook approach already wins and which formats have already been run into the ground — lean toward one the account hasn't tried if the diagnosis still fits. When a competitor set is monitored, its longest-running hooks are worth a look too — how long a competitor's hook has stayed live is the performance signal visible from outside.

## The four elements of a hook

Every hook is actually four things happening at once, and the strongest ones are deliberate about which is doing the work:

- **Text overlay** — the words on screen. Strongest as two short lines — the first opens the gap, the second flips it or raises the stakes; long lines clip on mobile, and "I" is a weak first word.
- **Sound** — the audio or spoken line.
- **Visual** — what's shown. Often carries more weight than the words.
- **Vibe** — the overall feel from lighting, color, pacing, aesthetic.

Not every hook needs all four active, but say which one is carrying it. The best hooks show rather than tell — changing the visual usually beats changing the text. The first second specifically is won by the visual and sound, not the spoken line, which can't say much in five or six words — keep the opening word count minimal so an arresting visual has room to land.

## Brand type and awareness stage

Same first branch as headlines: **problem/solution** brands can hook directly on the pain and the promised fix; **lifestyle** brands have to manufacture desire and identity instead, since there's no pain to name. Then awareness stage — unaware needs storytelling or education, problem-aware needs the problem named plainly, solution-aware needs the past failed attempt acknowledged before anything else, most-aware can go straight to proof and a direct call to act. For solution-aware audiences the hook lives or dies on the **mechanism** — what makes this one different from everything they already tried — and a mechanism reads strongest small and specific: a 7-second habit beats a 20-minute routine.

## Emotional driver

The dominant emotion behind the purchase points at which formats will land:

- **Fear** (health anxiety, missing out, fear of a wrong choice) → scam-trust-flip, controversy, question, myth-busting.
- **Aspiration** (wanting to be better, a transformation) → POV, viral-social-proof, speed-transformation, authority.
- **Exhaustion or frustration** (tried everything already) → investment-journey, comment-response, controversy.
- **Curiosity** (wants to know) → question-curiosity-gap, controversy, storytelling.
- **Tribal identity** (defining who I am) → demographic-qualification, POV.
- **Exhausted or wary cold audiences** (this shows up often on women 45+, but isn't limited to that group) — skip the high-intensity formats above and open with **permission**: release rather than activate ("You don't have to…", "you're allowed to stop…"). Calm converts here; pressure reads as more pressure and gets scrolled past.

Whatever the emotion, hold the same intensity through the hook rather than opening calm and spiking, or opening in high-intensity fear on a cold audience that hasn't earned that register yet — that mismatch is a common and avoidable miss, and it's worth naming because the reflex is to default to the highest-drama version of a line rather than the one that actually matches the audience.

## The format menu

Pick 2–4, never more — each with a real reason it fits this brand and this diagnosis, not a menu of everything that could apply.

**Authority / expert.** A credentialed or accomplished person delivers the message — a real one, never an invented credential. State the credential up front, use the vocabulary an actual practitioner would use (that register is what signals real expertise, not just the stated title), and show the knowledge in action rather than only asserting it. Best for trust-sensitive or high-consideration categories. Never use an expert outside their actual field — a claim just outside their credential undercuts the whole format.

**Comment-response.** Open on a real comment or objection, quoted, and answer it directly in conversation. Source the comment from the ad comments table or reviews — an invented comment reads as fabricated the moment it's slightly too convenient. Best for categories with a specific, nameable objection. Never sanitize the comment's actual wording into marketing language before responding to it.

**Comparison.** A direct side-by-side of two real options the buyer is actually weighing — cheap vs. expensive, old way vs. new, past self vs. now. Show both sides fairly before revealing the winner, and back the win with a real reason (a demonstration, a result, a mechanism), never an unsupported assertion. Never compare options the audience doesn't genuinely weigh, and never rig the setup so obviously it loses its contrast.

**Controversy / anti-traditional.** Names a specific, real, contested practice or belief and pushes against it, then resolves into the brand's actual alternative. Name the target precisely — "stop doing X" works, "the old way is wrong" doesn't. The counter-argument has to be genuinely defensible; manufacturing controversy where none exists backfires. Never leave the pushback unresolved — the alternative has to land.

**Conversational / native.** A casual mid-conversation opener that doesn't announce itself as an ad — "wait why is no one talking about this," "okay so I just found out." Don't name the product immediately; let the casual register hold for a beat first. Works best with a creator whose delivery reads genuinely unscripted; a stilted delivery kills the format outright.

**Demographic / qualification.** Calls out a specific identity directly in the opener so the right person self-selects immediately. Specificity is the entire mechanism — "working moms with kids under five" qualifies; "women" doesn't. Never use an identity marker the persona wouldn't actually claim for themselves.

**Educational / teach-in-timeframe.** Promises to teach something real within a stated time window — "give me 47 seconds" — and then actually delivers the lesson before any pitch. The timeframe should be a specific number, not "quickly." Never bait the promise of teaching and switch straight to a pitch; that breaks trust harder than not promising a lesson at all.

**How do I know if.** Opens by voicing the exact qualifying question the viewer is already silently asking — pulled from a real hesitation in reviews or comments, not invented — then answers it from the viewer's side, honestly naming who it's *for* and who it isn't. The honesty is the mechanism: a framework where everyone qualifies collapses the trust the format is built on.

**Investment / journey.** Names the real money or time already spent chasing a fix before finding this one — a specific, category-plausible number, not a round one that strains belief. Pair the number with the emotional cost, not just the dollar figure. Never inflate the investment for drama, and never borrow a journey shape from an unrelated category.

**Multi-hook stacking.** Stacks two or three *different* formats back to back — not the same format three times — each just a couple of seconds, with a visual transition marking the cut between them. Mostly a refresh tool for an existing ad that's losing steam, not a way to open a brand-new concept. Never exceed three stacked hooks.

**Native reveal (text thread / AI answer / notes).** Recreates a familiar phone surface — a chat thread reacting to a screenshotted result, an AI-answer screen, a notes page — and lets the brand emerge inside it: the thread is about the result until someone asks, and the brand is named once, late, by the peer. Written in real texting voice — lowercase, fragments; any bubble that sounds like ad copy gets cut. In the AI-answer variant the typed question is the hook and must be the target customer's verbatim question. The whole thing is a dramatization and stays honest as one: every claim inside must be true of the product, and it is never framed as a real customer's conversation. Best when a result can brag by itself — a number, a receipt, a before/after. Never name the brand early — that kills the reveal — and never use it where a casual text exchange undercuts a considered purchase.

**POV / emotional relatability.** "POV: you hate…" framing that drops the viewer into a highly specific, highly relatable moment, paired with a strong emotion word. The specificity of the scenario is what makes it work — a generic scenario applies to no one in particular. Never hold the POV without resolving it into the product as the response.

**Question / curiosity gap.** Opens on a question tied to a real pain or desire, pauses to let the viewer answer it mentally, then actually answers it. The gap mechanics: make the gap visible, make the answer feel worth having, delay it just long enough. Never pose a question with an obvious yes/no answer, and never leave it unanswered — an open loop the ad doesn't close reads as a bait. Two quieter ways the format dies: too vague — "something amazing happened" names no gap — and too complete, when the opening line answers its own question and leaves nothing to stay for.

**Reaction.** Opens on a genuine emotional reaction — before the cause is shown — held for a beat before cutting to the explanation. The reaction has to come from something the product actually produced; briefing a creator to perform an emotion rather than have one is the fastest way to read as fake. Never attach a big reaction to a benefit it doesn't actually match.

**Scam / trust-flip.** Opens on the tension of "I thought this was a scam," resolved quickly with real proof. Check the brand can actually carry this register — some categories and some voices can't use the word "scam" at all — and check platform compliance before writing it. Never leave the tension unresolved, and never let the framing accidentally imply the brand itself might be scamming.

**Speed and transformation.** Pairs a specific, real timeframe with visible before-and-after proof. The timeframe has to be realistic for the category — overpromising is detected and it costs trust. Compliance matters here specifically: health, weight, and medical categories carry real restrictions on before-and-after claims. Never use a vague timeframe, and never make a transformation claim that can't actually be shown.

**Storytelling / narrative.** Opens mid-story with a specific time marker and a specific person, not a generic beginning — "5 years ago my doctor told me…" lands harder than "I've always struggled." Best for unaware audiences who need the emotional runway before the product appears. Never make the product the hero of the opening line; it belongs later, as the resolution.

**Viral / social proof.** Leans on real, specific, verifiable momentum — an actual number, an actual platform reference — never invented buzz. A specific number ("over 2.5 million") reads as real; a round one ("millions and millions") reads invented. If the specific product isn't yet the viral thing, framing around the category's momentum still works. Never claim virality that doesn't exist — it's the easiest claim in this list to catch as false, and catching it costs the whole ad's credibility.

**Two formats that sit outside this list on purpose:**

- **Permission** is the format for exhausted or wary cold audiences (see Emotional driver above) — it doesn't get its own numbered slot because it's really the emotional-driver decision expressing itself as a hook, not a separate structural move.
- **Trigger words** ("scam," "secret," "obsessed," "never") aren't a format at all — they're an intensity layer applied inside whichever format is already chosen. Don't recommend a trigger word as its own hook.

## Choosing the set

In order: study the account's own hook history first. Lock the brand type. Filter by awareness stage, then by emotional driver. Cross off anything the brand has already run into the ground, and lean toward an untested format where the diagnosis still supports it. Land on 2–4 hooks, never more, and label each one plainly by what it's doing — a safe bet the account already knows works, an untapped format worth testing, or a sharper cut of something already running. A set that's every safe bet in a row is itself a miss: mix in at least one genuine test when a strong untested fit exists.

## Every hook has to clear

Relevance before raw attention — a hook that stops the wrong person's scroll is wasted spend. A clear promise inside the first three seconds. Zero confusion — one idea, fifth-grade language, no clickbait without a payoff. A nameable emotion — if you can't say which one, the concept isn't ready. And it has to look native to the platform, not styled like an ad from a few years ago.

Read every candidate against the writing-tells check before shipping it — same bar as headlines and scripts.
