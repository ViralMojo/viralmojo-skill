# Verdicts — the decision per creative

Every significant creative in a read gets one of four recommendations, plus two secondary outcomes. The verdict names the creative, the evidence, and the reason — never just the label.

## The spend × returns read

Cross a creative's spend against its returns-versus-target before anything else:

- **High spend, good returns** — the platform likes it and the economics hold: scale candidate.
- **High spend, poor returns** — the platform likes it but the economics don't; find the leak (funnel diagnosis) — fixable leaks get an iterate, structural ones a kill.
- **Low spend, good returns** — promising but untrusted by the platform; give it a fair test before concluding anything.
- **Low spend, poor returns** — kill quietly once it clears the spend floor; below the floor, keep watching.

## Kill

Signals, in combination: weak hook plus poor delivery; frequency climbing past ~2 with weakening returns; a week or two with no positive signal at meaningful spend.

**Always record why it died** — wrong persona, weak promise, wrong awareness stage, the open never stopped anyone. The reason is the lesson the spend paid for, and it is what stops the same idea from being re-tested blind. Don't kill early: below the meaningful-spend floor, *keep watching* beats a verdict.

## Iterate

The most common verdict: something in the creative works and something leaks. The funnel diagnosis names the leak; the iteration fixes **exactly one variable** — a new hook on the same body, the same script on a different face, a new visual under the same headline, a shorter cut of the same arc. One variable, because changing two attributes the result to neither.

Iteration milks a proven creative; it does not diversify the account. When every strong creative is an iteration of the same winner, the fix is a new direction, not another variant.

## Scale

Signals together: the platform keeps feeding it spend where allocation is freely given, returns hold at or above the brand's target, and frequency still has room. The verdict names the creative and why it earned more; **how** to scale it — budget moves, duplication, structure — is the user's media buying, not the read's. A scale verdict also burns the creative faster — more spend saturates its audience sooner — so when nothing is staged behind it, say so: budget scaled ahead of creative supply is fragile.

## Fatigue — reading the trajectory

Fatigue is a trend, never a snapshot. Calling it needs at least 4 weeks of history to compare against, and ideally a week-over-week breakdown, since fatigue accelerates as it progresses.

Signals to check, together: frequency climbing past ~1.5–2 (cold-audience lines — retargeting and small audiences run naturally higher); ROAS declining against the ad's own lifetime average; hook rate declining — the open losing its stopping power on people who've seen it before; hold rate declining — even when the hook still lands, more viewers have already seen the rest; CPM rising while reach holds flat or falls — paying more to reach the same people, and often the first signal to move; CPA drifting up against lifetime, even while still inside the brand's target.

One signal firing is a hint. Two is a pattern worth naming. Three or more, together with rising frequency, is fatigue — say so plainly.

The four stages, and what each earns:
- **Scaling** — frequency under ~1.5, signals flat or improving. Push more spend.
- **Mature plateau** — frequency 1.5–2, signals stable but not improving. Keep it running, and queue the next iteration rather than waiting for a break.
- **Early fatigue** — one or two signals declining, the rest still healthy. Iterate within the next few weeks, while the ad is still profitable enough to learn from.
- **Late fatigue** — three or more signals declining, frequency past 2, ROAS meaningfully below lifetime. Iterate now or kill — waiting only spends more against a saturated audience.

When fatigue calls for action, name which half died: stale execution earns a fresh execution of the same proven concept; an exhausted concept — its variants decaying with it — earns retirement toward a new direction, because no re-execution revives it.

Before naming fatigue from a frequency climb, check exclusion-list hygiene first — a stale exclusion list re-serves acquisition creative to existing customers, which reads as fatigue but is actually a targeting-hygiene problem the creative didn't cause.

## Open a new direction

The portfolio verdict, not a per-creative one. Call it when the account's winners share one DNA — same awareness stage, same format, same face — and new variants come in worse than the original while frequency climbs: the new ads are reaching the same audience pocket the winner already saturated, and the account is competing with itself. And the crowding is not only demand-side: Meta's retrieval layer clusters near-duplicate creatives and suppresses them before the auction — a stack of minor variations buys far less reach than its count suggests. More iteration digs the hole deeper.

What answers it is a concept that shares **nothing visible** with the current winner — a different awareness stage, format, and visual language — because only genuine divergence reaches people the current DNA cannot. Expect a new direction's early cost-per-result to run above the incumbent's while the platform learns it; say so up front so the user doesn't kill it for the crime of being new.

## Secondary outcomes

- **Keep watching** — under the spend floor or inside a conversion lag; name what would make it judgeable.
- **Archive as reference** — it didn't win but carries a reusable element (a hook that stopped scrolls on the wrong audience, a visual worth re-casting). Suggest banking the element as an idea; the banking itself is the user's call.

## The portfolio check

Close every read with the account-level look: a healthy account holds a few scales, many iterates, regular reasoned kills, and a steady stream of genuinely new concepts. If every verdict in the read says "iterate the winner," the account is converging on one pocket — say that in the read's own words.

## Comparing across creatives

When the question is which *kind* of creative wins — UGC against static, one format against another, one creator against another — a straight average or a best-ad comparison lies. Weigh each cohort by its total spend against its total customers, never by its single cheapest ad or a flat average across ads of very different sizes — one big winner can carry an entire cohort's average on its own. Cross-check the median against the spend-weighted number to catch that. Classify by what each ad carries — its media and its creative-format label — not an audience token sometimes baked into an ad's name, which leaves most spend unclassified, and say plainly what share of spend the comparison actually covers — a read built on 20% of spend is a different claim than one built on 80%.
